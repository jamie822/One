# Sales psychology doctrine

Operating rules for every site the studio builds for UK electricians and
solar installers. Each rule is an imperative with its evidence and source
inline. Where research is contested, the caveat is stated — build to the
caveat, not the headline. Compiled 2026-07-29 from web research; nothing
below is invented, and weakly-sourced vendor statistics were excluded.

## 1. First impressions and trust formation

1. **Win the first 50 milliseconds — the visual gut check happens before a
   single word is read.** Lindgaard et al. 2006 (Behaviour & Information
   Technology, ~1,000+ citations) showed visual-appeal judgments made at
   50ms correlate strongly with judgments at 500ms and persist via halo
   effect into credibility and usability judgments. Craft budget goes to
   the above-the-fold render first.
2. **Design IS the credibility argument.** Stanford Web Credibility Project
   (B.J. Fogg, 4,500+ participants): 46.1% of consumers assessed site
   credibility primarily on overall visual design — layout, typography,
   colour. Fogg: "if it doesn't look credible... it doesn't get a second
   test." A tradesperson's site that looks premium outranks a better
   tradesperson whose site looks 2009.
3. **Match trust requests to trust earned — never ask above the visitor's
   level.** Nielsen Norman Group's "pyramid of trust" (Hierarchy of Trust,
   nngroup.com): five commitment levels; demands at a higher level than the
   trust established shake the levels below. Concretely: no email-gated
   content or long forms before the page has proven relevance and
   credibility. Ask for a postcode before a phone number, a phone number
   before a survey booking.
4. **Perceived security is a feeling, not a certificate.** Baymard
   Institute (4,000+ US adults): ~1 in 5 abandonments cite not trusting the
   site; perceived security is a "gut feeling" driven by how secure the
   page LOOKS, and familiar consumer brands (Norton, Google) outperform
   technically identical seals. Translation for trades: NICEIC, MCS,
   TrustMark, Which? Trusted Trader logos work because homeowners
   recognise them — display real ones prominently, never obscure ones.

## 2. Cialdini's principles, applied to trade sites

5. **Lead with authority — it is the cheapest verified lever.** Cialdini
   (Influence, 1984; cxl.com/blog/cialdinis-principles-persuasion).
   Google's "Decoding Decisions" (2020, 310,000 simulated purchase
   journeys) ranked authority bias among the six most powerful shortcuts
   shoppers use in the "messy middle". For our clients: registration
   numbers (NICEIC/NAPIT, MCS), years trading, insurance, guarantees —
   named, numbered, and linked, not implied.
6. **Use reciprocity by giving genuinely useful things first.** Free solar
   savings calculators, honest cost guides, EICR explainers. Cialdini's
   reciprocity principle; CXL documents it as the mechanism behind
   content-led conversion. The gift must be real — a brochure-ware PDF is
   not a gift.
7. **Use commitment gradients: small yes before big yes.** Cialdini's
   consistency principle; Gonzales, Aronson & Costanzo 1988 (J. Applied
   Social Psychology) found energy auditors trained to induce small
   commitments (plus loss framing and vivid language) produced measurably
   more retrofit uptake in utility records. Structure solar funnels as:
   postcode check → rough estimate → free no-obligation assessment →
   quote. Never "Request a quote" as the only door.
8. **Social proof, authority, and scarcity beat brand loyalty when applied
   honestly and together.** Google's messy-middle experiments showed a
   competitor supercharged with the six biases (category heuristics,
   power of now, social proof, authority, scarcity, power of free) could
   dislodge an established favourite. We control the site: stack the
   honest versions of all six.

## 3. Social proof formats that measurably convert

9. **Get the first five reviews on the page — that is the single largest
   social-proof jump.** Spiegel Research Center, Northwestern (How Online
   Reviews Influence Sales, 2017): displaying five reviews vs zero raised
   purchase likelihood ~270%.
10. **Social proof matters MORE for expensive services, not less.** Same
    Spiegel study: reviews lifted conversion 190% for cheaper products but
    380% for higher-priced ones. A £6k solar quote page needs reviews more
    than a £90 fuse-board quote page does.
11. **Do not chase (or fake) a perfect 5.0.** Spiegel: purchase likelihood
    peaks around 4.2–4.7 stars and FALLS approaching 5.0 — perfect scores
    read as too good to be true. Show real aggregate ratings with review
    counts; never trim the imperfect ones (also illegal — see §8).
12. **Testimonials must be specific, attributed, and visual.** CXL's
    original eye-tracking research on social proof and Angie Schottmuller's
    CRAVENS model (via CXL): credible, relevant, attractive, visual,
    enumerated, nearby, specific. Format: real name, real town, photo
    where possible, and a concrete before/after ("EICR done in 2 days",
    "£68/month off the bill, 4.2kW system, Wrexham"). "Great service —
    Dave" persuades no one.
13. **"Nearby" is a proof multiplier for local trades.** Reviews and case
    studies from the visitor's own town outperform generic ones
    (Schottmuller/CXL "nearby" criterion). Build location pages with
    location-matched testimonials and job photos.

## 4. Pricing psychology

14. **Always anchor before revealing a price.** Ariely's anchoring
    experiments (Predictably Irrational; the social-security-number
    auction) show arbitrary anchors move willingness to pay. Practical
    form: show the typical market range or flagship package before the
    recommended one; state grant/0% VAT savings and 25-year output next to
    the install price so £7,000 lands against a £20k+ lifetime-benefit
    anchor, not against zero.
15. **Use a three-option structure with a deliberate middle.** Ariely's
    Economist experiment: with a decoy present, 84% chose the premium
    bundle; decoy removed, 68% chose the cheapest. Offer Good/Better/Best
    solar packages engineered so the target package is the obvious value.
16. **Round prices for the emotional purchase, precise numbers for the
    rational math.** Wadhwa & Zhang 2015 (Journal of Consumer Research):
    rounded prices "feel right" for feeling-driven purchases; non-rounded
    for cognition-driven ones. Solar package price: round (£6,500). The
    savings claim: precise (£1,137/year), because precision reads as
    calculated, not invented.
17. **Charm pricing (9-endings) is for discount contexts, not premium
    trades.** Field studies (Anderson & Simester, MIT/Chicago) found
    9-ending prices lift demand ~24–35% via left-digit anchoring — but the
    9-ending also signals "deal". A £5,999 solar system reads cheaper AND
    cheaper-quality. Default to round pricing for installs (per rule 16);
    reserve 9-endings for genuinely discounted service items if ever.
18. **"From £X" pricing must be honest and typical, and ALL mandatory
    costs must be in the headline price.** Drip pricing — revealing
    unavoidable fees later — is a banned practice under the UK DMCC Act
    2024 (unfair commercial practices regime in force 6 April 2025).
    Publish real from-prices anchored to a real, buyable spec.
19. **Show prices or ranges for standard work even when "it depends".**
    Transparency qualifies leads and builds the NN/g trust ladder (rule
    3); an empty "contact us for pricing" page pushes the messy-middle
    shopper (rule 8) back to comparison. Ranges + "what moves the price"
    satisfy category heuristics (Google, Decoding Decisions).

## 5. Loss aversion and framing

20. **Frame inaction as ongoing loss — with true numbers.** Kahneman &
    Tversky's prospect theory: losses weigh roughly twice gains. Gonzales,
    Aronson & Costanzo 1988 trained energy auditors to frame
    recommendations as loss ("you're losing money through that crack")
    and measured higher retrofit uptake in utility records. Solar copy:
    "Every sunny month without panels is ~£90 sent to your supplier" —
    provided the figure is defensible.
21. **For safety work, frame the risk being removed, not the product
    sold.** EICRs, fuse-board upgrades and EV-charger compliance are
    loss-prevention purchases; sell the avoided loss (fire, failed
    landlord cert, voided insurance) factually, without manufactured fear
    — exaggerated risk claims are misleading practice under DMCC/CPRs.

## 6. Urgency, choice architecture, and friction

22. **Only use urgency that is true, and explain WHY it is true.** CXL
    ("How to effectively create urgency"): urgency works when the deadline
    is real (booking calendar full, seasonal install window, tariff or
    grant deadline). Fake urgency is both a backfire (§8) and now illegal
    (rule 30).
23. **Cut options at the point of decision — but know the caveat.**
    Iyengar & Lepper 2000 (jam study): 24 options converted ~3% of
    stoppers, 6 options ~30%. Scheibehenne et al. 2010 meta-analysis (50
    experiments, N=5,036): mean choice-overload effect near zero with
    huge variance — overload bites when options are complex, unfamiliar,
    and preferences unformed. Solar for a first-time buyer is exactly
    that case. One primary CTA per page; three packages max; never a nav
    that competes with the phone number.
24. **Tell them they're free to say no.** Carpenter 2013 meta-analysis
    (Communication Studies, 42 studies, N≈22,000): adding "but you are
    free to decline" roughly doubles compliance, strongest when the
    decision is immediate. "Free assessment — no obligation, no follow-up
    calls unless you ask" is a measured persuasion device AND a
    differentiator in a sector known for pressure selling.
25. **Engineer for speed-to-lead; the website's job includes the first
    five minutes after submit.** Lead Response Management study (Oldroyd,
    MIT/InsideSales.com 2007): contacting a lead within 5 minutes vs 30
    yields ~21x qualification odds; HBR's 2011 audit of 2,241 firms found
    a 42-hour average response and 23% never responding. Build: instant
    auto-reply with a real human's name and a stated callback time,
    calendar self-booking, and lead alerts to the owner's phone. Google's
    "power of now" bias says stated response speed is itself persuasive —
    put "We answer within X" on the page only if it is true.

## 7. The two buyers

**Buyer A — the emergency caller** (power out, burning smell, landlord
cert due yesterday). A distress purchase: unplanned, resented, System-1,
made under perceived loss of control. Google/Ipsos: 76% of nearby-mobile
searchers visit/contact a business within 24 hours. They will not read.

26. **For Buyer A, optimise the 10-second path to a phone call.** Sticky
    click-to-call button, number in the hero, mobile-first. Levers that
    apply: 50ms visual trust (rule 1), instant authority badges (rule 5),
    glanceable aggregate rating + count (rules 9–11), locality signals
    ("Covering Wrexham & Chester — 45-minute response"), power of now.
    Levers that do NOT apply: scarcity/urgency (they already have
    maximal urgency — adding ours reads as vulture behaviour), long-form
    education, multi-step forms, package choices. One action: call.

**Buyer B — the considered solar investor** (£4k–£10k, weeks-to-months
journey, gathering 2–3+ quotes, looping through Google's messy middle of
exploration and evaluation, wary of a sector with a pressure-selling
reputation). Trade research consensus: "solar leads do not need pressure,
they need guidance over time."

27. **For Buyer B, sell the assessment, not the install.** Levers that
    apply: reciprocity via calculators and honest guides (rule 6),
    commitment gradient (rule 7), MCS/manufacturer authority (rule 5),
    specific local case studies with system size and payback (rules
    12–13), anchored three-tier pricing (rules 14–16), honest loss
    framing on bills (rule 20), BYAF no-pressure promise (rule 24),
    review volume — remember the 380% high-ticket effect (rule 10).
    Levers to use with extreme care: scarcity (only real install-slot or
    tariff deadlines); charm pricing (avoid, rule 17). Buyer B WILL
    refresh the page, compare quotes, and check Companies House — every
    claim must survive that.
28. **Serve both buyers from page one without letting either path pollute
    the other.** Emergency strip (call now) and considered path (learn /
    estimate / book) visually separated; the emergency element never uses
    countdowns, the solar element never uses "act now".

## 8. What backfires

29. **Fake countdowns and fake scarcity actively reverse conversion.**
    Industry testing consistently documents the "refresh test": a large
    share of shoppers (Growth Suite reports ~60%) reload to see if a
    timer resets; a resetting timer converts urgency into distrust of
    every future claim. Merchants mixing honest and fake scarcity end up
    worse off than those using neither (Growth Suite; scandiweb scarcity
    reviews). Honest timers in the same literature earn ~8–14% lifts —
    the tactic isn't broken, lying is.
30. **UK law now bans the classic dark patterns outright — DMCC Act 2024,
    in force 6 April 2025.** Banned or actionable: submitting or
    commissioning fake reviews; publishing reviews in a misleading way
    (cherry-picking, hiding incentivised reviews, suppressing negatives);
    false urgency such as fake countdown timers and untrue "limited
    availability"; drip pricing. CMA fining powers: up to 10% of global
    turnover or £300,000, and up to £150,000 for individuals (CMA208
    guidance; Lewis Silkin, Browne Jacobson, CMS analyses). This binds
    every review widget, "from" price, and urgency element we ship.
31. **Perfect ratings and scrubbed review walls backfire.** Spiegel
    (rule 11): near-5.0 averages depress purchase likelihood; and
    negative-review suppression is now a DMCC compliance risk. Show the
    4.8 with the one grumpy reply handled professionally.
32. **Trust seals cannot rescue an untrustworthy-looking page.** CXL's
    trust-seal research: unfamiliar seals do little, and seals fail when
    design, testimonials, and reviews are weak — the gut feeling (rule 4)
    dominates. Badges are seasoning, not structure.
33. **Best practices imported blind can fail — test against THIS
    audience.** CXL ("When conversion optimization best practices fail")
    documents reversals of textbook tactics in specific contexts; the
    choice-overload caveat (rule 23) is the canonical example. Doctrine
    here sets defaults; client A/B evidence overrides doctrine.
34. **Pressure tactics on distress buyers are the fastest trust killer.**
    A person in a powerless state (burst main, dead consumer unit) reads
    manufactured urgency as exploitation; the emergency page persuades by
    competence and speed signals only (rules 26, 28).

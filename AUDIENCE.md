# The Audience — What Makes Someone Ring an Electrician

Research July 2026 into UK homeowners, landlords and small businesses hiring
electrical work: how they decide, what frightens them, and what reassures them.

**This is the most important document for copywriting.** `RESEARCH.md` tells you
what competitors do. This tells you what the customer is actually thinking.

Evidence is graded: **[DATA]** = named survey, dataset or statute · **[SOFT]** =
real research but commercially interested or small sample · **[FOLKLORE]** =
widely repeated, no traceable source — **never put these in a deliverable.**

---

## 1. The finding that changes the brief

**For most visitors, the site is a verification channel, not a discovery
channel.**

- **69%** rely on word-of-mouth recommendations; **72%** had hired a
  tradesperson in the last 12 months (Checkatrade/Focaldata, 3,000 UK adults)
  **[DATA]**
- Only ~12% used a trade platform, ~18% Google, ~11% directories **[SOFT,
  probably pre-2020 — the Google share is certainly higher now]**

Someone has already been given a name. They are typing it into Google to check
the person is real, local, registered and not a disaster.

**The site's job is to survive scrutiny, not to make a cold pitch.** That single
sentence should shape every page. It means: verifiable specifics beat persuasive
language, and anything that looks evasive costs more than anything that looks
unpolished.

### They decide faster and compare less than the trade assumes

- Only **37%** got an itemised written quote. **23%** accepted a price with no
  breakdown. **3%** went on a verbal estimate **[DATA]**
- **38% of Gen Z hire the first tradesperson who quotes** (FMB/TrustMark, 2,000
  homeowners) **[DATA]**

The "three careful quotes" model is not what happens. **Whoever answers first,
sounds competent and is easy to verify tends to win.**

---

## 2. What rules you out instantly

**1. Nobody answers.** The best evidence here is old but solid: responding within
5 minutes made firms **21x more likely to qualify a lead** than waiting 30
minutes (MIT/InsideSales, 15,000+ leads) **[DATA]**. HBR audited 2,241 companies
and found an average first response of **42 hours**, with **23% never responding
at all** **[DATA]**.

> The most common "62% of calls go unanswered" figure is **[FOLKLORE]** — US
> vendor content with no traceable source. Don't quote it.

**This matters more than the website.** A site that generates calls into an
unanswered mobile is a wasted build. Every proposal should include either a
call-answering arrangement or an honest promise the client can actually keep.

**2. Missing or wrong contact details.** BrightLocal: **85% consider contact
information and opening hours important, 46% very important — ranking above
price, proximity and reviews.** **35% will go to a competitor** after finding
incorrect business information **[DATA]**.

This is the best-evidenced website finding in the entire brief, and it's also
the dullest. Phone number in the header, tappable, visible on a 375px screen
without scrolling.

**3. Stale or thin reviews.** Consumers read about **10 reviews over 13 minutes
45 seconds**, and **73% only trust reviews written in the last month**
(BrightLocal 2025) **[DATA]**.

Ten detailed, dated reviews from the last three months beat two hundred undated
ones. **Review recency is a maintenance discipline, not a launch task** — and a
legitimate reason for the monthly plan.

**4. Anonymity.** No name, no face, no address.

## 3. What they're actually afraid of

### Fear is suppressing demand, measurably

- Consumer fear of cowboy builders costs the UK economy roughly **£10bn a year
  in work never commissioned**. **One in five who need work done find reasons to
  delay it** (FMB/OnePoll, 2,000 homeowners) **[DATA]**
- **Only 52% of consumers trust the tradespeople they actually hired**
  (Checkatrade/Focaldata) **[DATA]** — that's trust *after* the hire
- **63%** had suffered dishonest operators: poor workmanship 38%, failure to meet
  spec 25%, incomplete jobs 24% **[DATA]**
- Citizens Advice logged **22,500+ rogue trader complaints Jan–Oct 2024, up 24%
  year on year** **[DATA]**

**The average spend repairing botched work is £8,729** — but **Gen Z homeowners
average £20,534 versus £5,128 for over-60s** **[DATA]**. Younger buyers hire
faster, verify less, and get burned harder. If the client targets first-time
homeowners, they're talking to the most anxious and least equipped segment.

### The anxiety nobody addresses

**Only 22% of women say having a tradesperson alone in their home has never made
them feel unsafe — against 59% of men** (YouGov) **[DATA]**.

Roughly four in five women have felt unsafe at least sometimes. **Almost no
electrician's website acknowledges this exists.**

For that reader, these are not decoration — they are the deciding factor:

- A real photograph and a first name, so they know who's coming
- "We'll text you a photo of the engineer before he arrives"
- ID cards, uniform, marked van
- DBS checks, if the client has them
- A named person to ring if something feels wrong

This is the single biggest untapped trust opportunity in the market.

### The four buyers, and their different fears

| Buyer | Dominant fear | Kills the enquiry | Wins it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emergency** | *Will anyone come, and how badly will I be stitched up when I've no choice?* | No answer; no out-of-hours policy; no call-out fee stated | Human answers; call-out fee and hourly rate stated **before** dispatch; realistic ETA; "we'll tell you the cost before we start" |
| **Planned rewire** | Cost overrun, damage, someone vanishing mid-job, an uncertified mess | Vague pricing; no comparable jobs shown; no written scope | Itemised quote; day-by-day timeline; photos including **making-good**; named guarantee; payment schedule without a big deposit |
| **Landlord** | Being sold remedials they don't need | Any hint of a bait price plus inevitable extras; slow certificate | Fixed price per property; stated turnaround; **"we quote remedials separately and you're free to use anyone"** |
| **Business** | Downtime and paperwork that won't satisfy an insurer or the HSE | No commercial credentials; no out-of-hours option | Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 duty; phased testing; documentation insurers accept; PL figure |

**Statutory grounding:** private rented homes in England need an EICR every five
years, with a copy to the tenant **within 28 days**; councils can fine **up to
£30,000 per breach** **[DATA — statutory]**. And **one in five landlords doesn't
know EICRs are a legal requirement; 21% don't know about the fines** (Direct
Line) **[DATA]**.

That last statistic matters: **the information gap is real and documented**, so
telling landlords about it is genuinely useful, not fear-selling.

## 4. What reassures them, ranked by evidence

### Strong evidence

1. **Complete, correct, prominent contact details.** Top-ranked factor, above
   price and reviews **[DATA]**.
2. **Recent, detailed reviews.** 73% only trust reviews from the last month
   **[DATA]**.
3. **Responding to reviews, including bad ones.** Businesses that started
   responding got **12% more reviews and ratings rose 0.12 stars** (HBR study of
   TripAdvisor) **[DATA]**. Over 80% think businesses should respond **[DATA]**.
   There's a documented downside to responding to *everything* mechanically —
   so: all negatives, a sample of positives.
4. **Don't chase a perfect 5.0.** Northwestern's Spiegel Research Center found
   **purchase likelihood peaks at 4.2–4.5 stars, and products rated 4.7–5.0 are
   *less* likely to be purchased than those rated 4.2–4.7** **[DATA]**. A few
   4-star reviews with a good reply underneath are more persuasive than a wall
   of unbroken fives.
5. **Showing prices.** Nielsen Norman Group: **price is the number one
   information need**; users get frustrated and leave sites that hide it, and
   **view companies that hide costs as evasive and untrustworthy** **[DATA]**.
   This is the strongest available evidence against "call for a quote" as the
   only pricing information.
6. **Short forms — but it's about perceived effort, not field count.** Unbounce's
   data shows conversion highest at one field, levelling between four and seven,
   then *rising* again at ten. HubSpot's 4→3 fields = +50% is **[SOFT]**. Default
   for an electrician: **name, phone, postcode, one free-text box.** Postcode
   earns its friction because it qualifies.

### Plausible, weak formal evidence

7. **Real photographs of the actual person, named.** Stock photos of a "team"
   that visitors recognise from other sites create immediate distrust **[SOFT]**.
   Combined with the YouGov safety data, I'd rate a real face as one of the
   highest-leverage items on the site — while being honest that **no controlled
   study quantifies it for trade websites.**
8. **Accreditation logos** work when placed **at the point of anxiety** — next to
   the form or the price, not orphaned in the footer **[SOFT]**.
9. **Insurance figures.** "£5m public liability" beats "fully insured" because
   it's specific and falsifiable. **[No trade-specific study found.]**
10. **Guarantees that name their backer.** Insurance-Backed Guarantees exist
    because a company can cease trading and leave the guarantee worthless
    **[DATA — scheme rules]**.

### Folklore — never put these in a deliverable

- "Click-to-call increases conversions 20–40%" **[FOLKLORE]**
- "Before/after photos boost conversion 83%" **[FOLKLORE — traced to a
  body-composition software blog]**
- "62% of calls go unanswered" **[FOLKLORE]**
- "78% of UK consumers prefer messaging over calling" **[FOLKLORE — contradicted
  by YouGov]**

Build before/after galleries anyway. Electrical work is largely invisible, so a
tidy consumer unit, square cable runs and properly made-good plaster do real
work. Just don't cite a fake number for it.

### One product change worth knowing

On **20 October 2025 Google consolidated "Google Guaranteed", "Google Screened"
and "License Verified by Google" into a single "Google Verified" badge, and
discontinued the Google Guarantee money-back guarantee** **[DATA]**. Don't build
a site around a badge that no longer exists.

## 5. The accreditation problem

**The badges mean far less to consumers than the trade believes.**

- Public awareness of Part P has been cited at **14%** **[DATA, but traces to
  around the 2013 Part P changes — do not present as a current figure]**
- **NAPIT told Parliament in written evidence that consumer awareness of
  "Competent Persons" remains low** **[DATA — the scheme operator conceding it]**
- **A quarter of consumers don't check credentials at all** **[DATA]**
- Consumers understand Gas Safe. There is no equivalent mental model for
  electrical work **[SOFT, but consistent with the Part P figure]**

**So a row of logos is doing almost no work.** Each badge needs a sentence
translating it into something the reader already cares about:

> **NICEIC Approved Contractor.** An independent assessor inspects our work every
> year. It also means we can self-certify to Building Control — so you get the
> Building Regulations compliance certificate your solicitor will ask for when
> you sell.

That turns a meaningless logo into a document they'll need at a moment they
already worry about.

### Three things worth actually teaching

**Part P and notification.** Notifiable work includes new circuits, consumer unit
replacements, and work in bathrooms. **The homeowner is ultimately legally
responsible**, not the electrician. Solicitors require Building Regulations
certificates for notifiable work since 2005, and their absence **can delay or
jeopardise a house sale** **[DATA]**. This is the most useful thing to explain on
the whole site.

**EICR vs EIC.** An EICR reports on the condition of an *existing* installation;
an EIC certifies *new work*. Consumers routinely conflate them.

**C1/C2/C3 codes.** This is where fear-selling happens and where transparency is
the sharpest differentiator. **C3 means "improvement recommended" and does not
make a report unsatisfactory.** A page saying so is a direct inoculation against
the electrician down the road who quotes remedials for every C3.

### If you mention fire risk, use the real numbers

Electrical sources cause **around 53.4% of accidental dwelling fires in England**
— roughly **14,186 a year** **[DATA — Home Office, via Electrical Safety First]**.

But **faulty appliances and leads are the largest single sub-category at ~25.9%**
**[SOFT]**.

**Most electrical fires start with appliances and leads, not consumer units.**
That is inconvenient for the "your fuse board is a fire risk" script — and
saying it out loud is a strong, honest differentiator.

## 6. Fear-selling — the argument, corrected

**Do not claim fear appeals don't work. They do.**

Tannenbaum et al. (2015), *Psychological Bulletin* — meta-analysis of 127
articles, 248 samples, N≈27,372 — found a **positive effect on attitudes,
intentions and behaviour (d = 0.29)** and stated there are **"no identified
circumstances under which they backfire"** **[DATA]**.

Anyone in the trade using scare tactics has probably watched them work. A pitch
built on "fear doesn't work" will be dismissed by exactly the people you need to
convince. **The real case has three legs.**

### Leg 1 — that meta-analysis doesn't license commercial fear-selling

Its samples are overwhelmingly health and social marketing, where the threat is
**genuine**, the communicator has **no financial interest** in the recommended
action, and the action **actually reduces the risk**. None of those hold when an
electrician standing in your hallway says a working installation is about to
burn your house down and he can fix it today for £900.

"Fear works when the threat is real and the fix is real" is not "fabricated
threats work."

### Leg 2 — reactance, and the priming problem

When people feel coerced they resolve the discomfort by **derogating the
source** — finding reasons the messenger isn't credible — rather than complying
(Hastings, Stead & Webb) **[DATA]**.

The trade context maximises this risk. **Only 52% trust the tradesperson they
already hired.** A fear pitch lands on pre-existing suspicion, not a neutral
prior. **The same message that persuades a stranger in a public health campaign
confirms a suspicion in a hallway.**

### Leg 3 — several common forms are illegal

**Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008:**

- **Reg 5, misleading actions** — false information *or* an overall presentation
  likely to deceive. "Your consumer unit is a fire risk" when it's a C3.
- **Reg 6, misleading omissions** — not saying a recommendation is optional.
- **Reg 7, aggressive practices** — coercion or undue influence impairing freedom
  of choice. "It needs doing today", said in a kitchen.
- **Schedule 1 banned practices** — automatically unfair, no need to prove
  detriment. Includes **falsely stating something is available only for a very
  limited time to elicit an immediate decision**, and bait-and-switch.
- Consumers get **rights to redress**: unwind the contract, a discount, damages.

**Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024**, in force **6 April
2025**:

- **The CMA can fine directly, up to 10% of global turnover**, without going to
  court **[DATA]**
- **Drip pricing prohibited** — a headline price with mandatory charges added
  later. A "from £X" that omits an unavoidable call-out fee or VAT is exactly
  this.
- **Fake reviews prohibited**, including failing to take reasonable steps to
  prevent them.

**CMA on urgency:** the **Emma Sleep case**, endorsed by the High Court, confirmed
false countdown timers and misleading urgency are illegal. Businesses must
**hold evidence substantiating urgency claims** **[DATA]**.

**ASA / CAP Code rule 4.2:** *"Marketing communications must not cause fear or
distress without justifiable reason; if it can be justified, the fear or distress
should not be excessive."* CAP explicitly allows fear appeals for genuine safety
products — **but not excessive ones**, and never a shocking claim used merely to
attract attention **[DATA]**.

**The line, quotable to a client:** you are permitted — encouraged, even — to
tell people about a genuine danger. You are not permitted to manufacture time
pressure, exaggerate, or omit that a recommendation is optional.

### The test

| | Legitimate | Manufactured |
|---|---|---|
| Is the risk real and specific to this installation? | Evidenced by inspection, with a recorded code | Asserted generically, applied to every property |
| Would you say it if you weren't doing the work? | Yes | No |
| Is the deadline real? | Statutory date, tenancy start, live fault | "Today only", "prices go up Friday" |
| Free to get another opinion? | Told explicitly they are | Discouraged, or the offer expires first |
| Is the fix proportionate? | C1 act now · C2 act soon · C3 optional | Everything becomes a board change |
| Is the price complete? | Total, inc. call-out and VAT | Extras appear later (unlawful) |

### Same message, three ways

**Consumer unit**

❌ *"Old fuse box? It's a fire waiting to happen. Book today — only 2 slots
left."*
Unevidenced severity (reg 5), false scarcity (Schedule 1), excessive fear (CAP
4.2).

⚠️ *"Electrical faults cause over half of house fires. Is your fuse board putting
your family at risk? Call now."*
True statistic, deceptively deployed — most of those fires start with appliances.
The rhetorical question implies a risk nobody has assessed. Caught by the
overall-presentation limb of reg 5.

✅ *"If your fuse board has rewireable fuses or no RCD protection, it doesn't meet
current standards — worth knowing about, not panicking about. It isn't illegal
and it doesn't need doing today. What it does mean is that if a fault develops,
the board may not cut the power fast enough. An EICR (£XXX, about two hours)
tells you exactly where you stand, in writing, with every finding coded. If
everything's fine we'll tell you that and you'll owe us nothing more. If it
isn't, we'll quote the work separately and you're free to take that quote
anywhere."*

**Landlord EICR**

❌ *"URGENT: Landlords face £30,000 fines. Don't risk it."*

✅ *"If you let property in England you need a valid EICR every five years, and
tenants must have a copy within 28 days. Councils can fine up to £30,000. Not
sure when yours expires? Send us the address and we'll tell you — no charge.
£XXX per property including the certificate, back within 48 hours. Anything
needing remedial work we quote separately and itemised, and you're under no
obligation to use us."*

Same statute, same number, no manufactured urgency — and the differentiator
answers the landlord's actual fear.

**Emergency**

❌ *"24/7 emergency electrician — FREE call out!"* then a £150 minimum on arrival.
Drip pricing, directly actionable under the DMCCA.

✅ *"Emergency call-out: £XX covering the first hour, including VAT, day or night.
If we can't fix it in that hour we'll tell you what it'll cost before carrying
on."*

### The principle

**Replace urgency with certainty.**

The anxious buyer doesn't lack motivation — one in five is *actively delaying*
work they know needs doing. They lack the ability to tell a good electrician from
a bad one.

**The persuasive lever is not "act now". It is "here is exactly what will happen,
what it will cost, and how you can check I'm telling the truth."** Every piece of
specificity — a price, a name, a photo, a registration number they can look
up — reduces the perceived risk of picking up the phone. That is the entire
conversion mechanism for this category.

## 7. Conversion mechanics

### Benchmarks — with a warning

**No audited, methodologically transparent benchmark exists for UK trade site
conversion.** Everything available is aggregator content. Directionally
**[SOFT]**: 1–2% unoptimised, 2–5% healthy, 5%+ strong, ~7.5% for paid search to
a local service landing page.

**Conversion rate is meaningless without traffic source.** Someone arriving from
a GBP after reading reviews converts entirely differently from a blog visitor.
Report by source or don't report.

There's a commercial opportunity here: a partnership measuring the same trust
signals across a portfolio of electrician sites would own the only real data in
the UK market.

### Mobile

~64% of global web traffic and ~73% of Google traffic is mobile **[SOFT]**.

The consequence isn't "make it responsive". It's: **the phone number must be
tappable and visible in the first viewport on a 375px screen without scrolling,
and stay reachable as a sticky element.**

### Channels

- **Phone primary** — YouGov confirms phone and email remain most used, with
  older consumers strongly preferring phone **[DATA]**
- **WhatsApp prominent secondary** — one in three Brits would prefer SMS or
  WhatsApp for service contact **[SOFT]**, and it serves both the under-35s and
  the anxious-at-home segment who don't want to speak to a stranger
- **Form third**
- **Live chat: avoid** on a one-van operation. An unanswered widget is worse than
  none, and a chatbot on a safety-critical trade site reads as corporate distance

### Pricing pattern

Given NN/g's finding and the DMCCA drip-pricing rules:

- **Publish real fixed prices where they exist** — EICR by bedroom count, EV
  charger install, socket, call-out fee
- **Where genuinely variable, publish the hourly rate and call-out fee** and say
  what makes it vary
- **Never a "from" price that omits a mandatory charge** — now directly
  enforceable
- The framing that resolves the tension: *"Here's what most jobs like yours cost.
  We won't know your exact price until we've seen it, and we'll tell you before
  we start."*

### Form defaults

Name, phone, postcode, one free-text box. **Don't ask** for address, preferred
contact time, how they heard about you, budget range, or a fifteen-option job
dropdown.

Set expectations **at the form** — *"We reply within 2 working hours, 7am–7pm"* —
then honour it. An unkept response promise is worse than none, and arguably a
misleading action.

## 8. Gaps — stated honestly

1. **No reliable UK figure** for how many electricians a homeowner contacts, or
   how long they take. The "3–5 quotes" number is a platform artefact.
2. **No current figure** for consumer awareness of NICEIC/NAPIT/TrustMark/Part P.
   The 14% is real but roughly a decade old. Safest line: NAPIT's own
   parliamentary evidence conceding awareness is low.
3. **No research on consumer understanding of C1/C2/C3 codes.** Probably near
   zero, but that's inference.
4. **No controlled study of trust-signal conversion lift on trade sites** — no
   real-photo test, no badge test, no insurance-figure test. Everything in that
   space is assertion.
5. **Emergency-buyer research is almost entirely US.** UK behaviour under
   electrical emergency is undocumented.
